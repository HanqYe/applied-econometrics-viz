Cross-section data: each row is a person with two predictors, X₁ and X₂.
Each column of X spans a line in ℝn. Col(X₁) and Col(X₂) are not orthogonal — the columns are correlated.
The outcome y lives in the same space.
Project y onto Col(X₁) and Col(X₂) separately. Mark each projection red — they are β̃₁x₁ and β̃₂x₂.
Vector-add the two projections (parallelogram rule). The sum β̃₁x₁ + β̃₂x₂ misses y — there's a gap.
Now suppose Col(X₁) and Col(X₂) were orthogonal. Project y again — mark these projections blue.
Vector-add again. With orthogonal regressors the parallelogram is a rectangle, and β̂₁x₁ + β̂₂x₂ lands exactly on y.
y still decomposes uniquely as β̂₁ x₁ + β̂₂ x₂ — slide along each axis to read off the coefficients.
Simple projection gives β̃; joint OLS gives β̂. They are different lengths.
The problem is non-orthogonality. Can we build an orthogonal direction out of what we have?
Drop X₂ perpendicular to Col(X₁) → residual v; project y on v → β̂₂. Symmetrically: drop X₁ perpendicular to Col(X₂) → residual u; project y on u → β̂₁.
In 3D, y projects onto the plane spanned by Col(X₁) and Col(X₂) — call it ŷ. Within that plane, we residualize X₂ against Col(X₁) to get v, then project ŷ onto v.
We built a new orthogonal basis {Col(X₁), v} from the original {Col(X₁), Col(X₂)}. In the new basis, simple regressions just work — that's the FWL theorem.
